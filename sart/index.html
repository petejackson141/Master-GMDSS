        Key facts

        9 GHz (X-band)
        A radar SART answers only 3 cm (X-band) radar, 9.2–9.5 GHz. An S-band (10 cm) radar does not trigger it.
        12 dots
        On the searcher's radar it shows as a line of 12 dots about 0.6 nm apart, pointing away from the SART along its bearing. The SART is at the dot nearest the radar's own ship.
        Arcs and circles
        Within about 1 nm the dots spread into arcs. Very close, they become circles round the centre of the screen.
        Range
        At least 5 nm from a ship's radar (scanner 15 m up, SART 1 m above the sea). About 30 nm from an aircraft at 3,000 ft. Height matters: hold it at least 1 m up, on its pole.
        Battery
        96 hours on standby, then at least 8 hours answering radar. Replace by the expiry date on the label.
        Indicator
        The light or beeper changes when a radar reaches the SART, so survivors know a ship or aircraft is close.
        Radar range
        Use the 6 or 12 nm range so the line of dots fits on the screen. On 3 nm the SART may be off the edge.
        Carriage
        SOLAS cargo ships of 300–500 GT carry one SART; passenger ships and cargo ships of 500 GT and over carry two. An AIS-SART (ID starting 970) can be carried instead.
        Testing
        Test briefly with your own X-band radar: you should see rings round the centre of the screen. Keep tests short, as nearby ships see the rings too.
        Switched on by mistake
        Switch it off. Other ships' radars will show it as a distress signal.
